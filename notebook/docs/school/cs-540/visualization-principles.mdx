# Visualization principles: chart choice, audience, decluttering, and data stories

> **Source:** CS-540 Modules 4 to 9, the zyBook chapters on visualization and storytelling (which teach Knaflic's *Storytelling with Data* lessons), and Kenn Adams on the story spine. Full references at the bottom.
>
> **Saved via:** End-of-course notes.

## The idea

A chart is for a specific person and a specific decision. Pick the display that fits the data and the relationship, cut everything that doesn't help, point at the one thing that matters, and put it in a story with a beginning, middle, and end.

## Knaflic's six lessons (the core of Modules 7 and 8)

1. **Understand the context.** Who is the audience, what do they need to know or do, and what data backs it up.
2. **Choose an appropriate display.** Simple text for one or two numbers, lines for continuous data, bars for categories with a zero baseline.
3. **Eliminate clutter.** Remove what doesn't add information. Use alignment, white space, and contrast.
4. **Focus attention where you want it.** Color, size, and position, used sparingly. Test it: where are your eyes drawn first?
5. **Think like a designer.** Highlight the important stuff, cut distractions, build a visual hierarchy, label in plain text.
6. **Tell a story.** Beginning (plot), middle (twists), end (call to action).

## Exploratory vs. explanatory

| | Exploratory | Explanatory |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who it's for | Me, maybe one teammate | People who didn't do the analysis |
| Goal | Find out what's in the data | Get one point across and drive an action |
| Charts | Many, rough, fast, mostly thrown away | One or two, designed and annotated |
| Fails when | I chase noise or stop too early | I overstate the data or bury the point |

Knaflic's image: exploration is opening a hundred oysters to find a couple of pearls. The explanatory version shows the pearls.

## Choosing a chart

Start from the relationship you want to show, then the data type.

| I want to show | Use | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One or two key numbers | Big text with a short label | A chart for the sake of it |
| Change over time | Line chart; indexed lines (base = 100) for different units | Bars for long continuous series, dual y-axes |
| Compare categories | Bar chart, horizontal and sorted when labels are long | Pie and donut charts, 3D |
| Parts of a whole | Sorted bars or 100% stacked bars | Pies with many slices |
| Distribution | Histogram, box plot | Means alone |
| Relationship between two numbers | Scatter plot; bubble size for a third variable | Implying causation |
| Matrix of values (correlation, cluster profiles) | Heatmap | Raw tables of 100 numbers |
| Hierarchy or many groups | Overview chart plus drill-down | One chart with every level at once |
| Many lines | Highlight one or two, gray the rest, or small multiples | Spaghetti graphs |

Rules that came up again and again:

- Bars start at zero.
- Use a log scale when values span orders of magnitude and relative change matters.
- Normalize before comparing places of different sizes (per resident, per trip, per 1,000 tons).

## Designing for the audience

In Module 9 the same analysis served two groups, so the charts split:

| | Leadership / executives | The team that acts (carrier sales and operations) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Needs | The headline and why it matters to the business | Where to act and on what |
| Charts | Ranked bar of top origin states; indexed cost lines | Bubble scatter of freight vs. carrier trucks; fleet-size bars; equipment setup grouped bars |
| Design | Few marks, national framing, the finding in the title | State labels, ratios they can act on, a reference line, highlighted states |

The same Module 4 lesson from the other side: a technically complete multi-line chart can still be the wrong pick for non-expert policymakers. Choose for the reader, then justify each refinement by what that reader needs.

## Decluttering checklist

- [ ] Remove borders, heavy gridlines, and the top and right spines
- [ ] Gridlines only on the value axis, and light
- [ ] Label data directly (end of line, on the bar) instead of a legend when you can
- [ ] Round numbers to what the reader needs
- [ ] Left-align the title and put it where the eye starts
- [ ] Put the source and any caveats in a small gray note at the bottom
- [ ] Cut anything that's left over and doesn't support the claim

## Titles that state the claim

Name the finding, not the axes.

| Weak | Strong |
| --- | --- |
| "Truck freight by state, 2024" | "Five states ship 48% of U.S. vehicle freight by truck, but carrier counts do not follow the freight" |
| "Fleet size distribution" | "About half of vehicle-hauling carriers run a single truck" |
| "Diesel vs. PPI" | "Since January 2020, truckload rates are up 69% and diesel is up 105%" |

Horizontal logic test: read only the titles in order. They should tell the whole story by themselves.

## Labels and annotation

- Axis labels say the unit: "million tons," "share of carriers," "Index, January 2020 = 100."
- Annotate the point you're talking about (the last value on a line, the highlighted states).
- Define jargon once in a note (power unit, kt, PPI).
- Hover tooltips should show real counts, not the normalized 0 to 1 values used for plotting.

## Color for emphasis

- Gray for context, one strong color for the thing that matters.
- Keep one color meaning across every chart in a report (in 9-1: blue for carriers, orange for highlighted states, gold for diesel, gray for context).
- Use colorblind-safe palettes (Okabe-Ito, seaborn `"colorblind"`). Don't rely on red against green.
- Diverging colormaps only for data with a meaningful center (correlation around 0). Sequential colormaps for values that only go up.
- Color should never be the only way to read the chart. Back it with labels or position.

## Overview and detail

- Overview first: the national or global picture in one simple chart.
- Detail on demand: dropdowns, a range slider, or a second chart that zooms into one part.
- In 9-1 this was the "multi-level" requirement: a high-level overview chart for leadership and detailed insight charts for the team.
- In Module 6 it was a global total with a drill-down by continent and an overview plus detail layout.

## Accessibility (Module 6)

- Colorblind-safe palette, enough contrast, readable font sizes.
- Plain-language titles and labels.
- Don't distort with scale tricks: truncated bar axes, mismatched dual axes, or uneven intervals.
- Use an appropriate map projection for geographic charts.

## Data stories with the Pixar story spine

Kenn Adams created the story spine in 1991 as an improv exercise. It's often called the Pixar story spine. Each line is a prompt:

1. **Once upon a time...** the normal situation
2. **Every day...** what keeps happening
3. **But one day...** the change
4. **Because of that...** the first consequence
5. **Because of that...** the next consequence
6. **Until finally...** the resolution, which is your recommendation
7. **And ever since then...** what's different afterward

How the 9-1 story used it (public data only):

| Beat | 9-1 content | Chart |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Once upon a time | Car shipping runs on tens of thousands of small carriers. 52% run one truck | Fleet-size bars |
| Every day | About 193 million tons of vehicles and parts move by truck, and nearly half starts in five states | Ranked origin-state bars |
| One day | Costs jumped: diesel up 105%, truckload rates up 69% since January 2020 | Indexed cost lines |
| Because of that | Every empty mile costs more, so where carriers are based compared to where cars are matters | |
| Because of that | Carriers track population (r = 0.84) more than freight (r = 0.57). KY has 373 trucks per 1,000 kt vs. 1,710 nationally | Bubble scatter |
| Until finally | Focus carrier recruiting on KY, MI, AR, AL, and SC, and build tools that fit how carriers describe their equipment | Equipment setup bars |

Guardrails:

- Every "because of that" claims a cause. Keep it only if the data supports that step. Otherwise say "at the same time."
- Lead with the Big Idea when the audience might miss it at the end.
- Write the 3-minute story first. If you can't say it in three minutes, the charts won't fix it.
- Storyboard on paper before building slides or charts.

## Key takeaways

- Context first: who, what they need to do, and what data backs it.
- Pick the chart from the relationship and the data type, not from habit.
- Declutter, then highlight one thing with color or size.
- Titles state the finding. The titles alone should tell the story.
- Different audiences get different charts from the same analysis.
- A story structure makes a finding stick, but every causal step still needs evidence.
