Before completing this activity, I tended to use the word *model* to describe the flagship generative artificial intelligence (AI) products that I encounter in my work. When I thought about models, I thought about Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, and the other large language models that receive most of the attention. My conversation with GitHub Copilot, along with the assigned reading, forced me to step back from that narrow definition. ## The Relationship Between Machine Learning and Deep Learning

Machine learning is a broad area of AI in which systems learn patterns from data to make predictions, classifications, or decisions. Deep learning is a specialized form of machine learning that uses neural networks with multiple layers (zyBooks, n.d., Section 1.1). In other words, the two are related, but they are not interchangeable. All deep learning systems are machine learning systems, while many machine learning systems do not use deep neural networks. This distinction matters because choosing a model should begin with the problem being solved rather than with the popularity of a particular technology.

The different approaches also make different tradeoffs. Traditional machine learning models, such as decision trees, regression models, support vector machines, and clustering algorithms, can be effective when data is structured and the important features are already understood. They may require a person to decide which features should be supplied to the model, but they can need less data and computing power and can be easier to interpret. Deep learning models can learn increasingly complex representations from data with less manual feature engineering. This makes them especially valuable for unstructured inputs such as natural language, audio, and images. However, that capability generally comes with greater demands for training data and computing resources, as well as outputs that may be more difficult to explain. The course text reinforces this tradeoff by explaining that deep learning can capture complicated relationships while making those relationships difficult to interpret (zyBooks, n.d., Section 1.8).

The conversation was eye-opening because it changed the question I would ask if I wanted to build a model. I previously might have started by asking which major language model I should use. I would now begin by asking what kind of problem I am solving, what data is available, whether the result must be explainable, and what level of cost and complexity is justified. A relatively simple classification or prediction problem may not benefit from a deep neural network at all. A problem involving language generation or the interpretation of large amounts of unstructured data is more likely to justify deep learning. Generative AI is therefore not a replacement for the rest of machine learning. It is one highly visible application of deep learning within a much larger collection of AI methods.

This broader understanding also connects to how I view current models in my professional work. Today's models are the most capable I have used, yet they are probably the least capable models we will use from this point forward. Their continued improvement makes it difficult to decide where developers should invest their learning time. Strong programming foundations still matter because they allow a developer to evaluate an output instead of accepting it blindly. At the same time, I am finding that architecture, design patterns, system boundaries, and verification are becoming increasingly valuable skills.

For example, I recently used an AI coding assistant while creating AWS infrastructure with the Cloud Development Kit (CDK) and TypeScript. Once I had designed, tested, and verified a reusable construct, much of the remaining work involved directing the assistant to follow that established pattern in another continuous integration and continuous delivery pipeline. I could then verify the result by reviewing the code and confirming that the expected infrastructure was created. The model accelerated the repetitive implementation, but I was still responsible for defining the pattern and deciding how to validate it. That experience illustrates why understanding the relationship among models is practical rather than merely theoretical. The best model is not automatically the largest or newest one; it is the model and workflow that fit the task, its risks, and the available means of verification.

## GitHub Copilot's Effectiveness

My evaluation of GitHub Copilot is influenced by approximately a year and a half of using it in my professional work. Overall, I consider it an effective tool, especially inside Visual Studio Code. It places the conversation close to the code, exposes the tools and context available to the model, and helps users understand that every enabled tool consumes some of the model's limited context. That visibility was particularly useful while I was learning how agentic coding tools operated. As a research partner in this activity, Copilot was also effective at quickly organizing concepts, comparing approaches, and giving me a starting point for deeper questions. Most importantly, its answers prompted me to recognize that I had been using the term *model* too narrowly.

However, Copilot's effectiveness cannot be judged only by the quality of the underlying language model. Copilot is a harness: it manages the prompts, instructions, context, tools, and model that produce the final response. A capable model can appear less capable when its harness provides incomplete context or imposes limitations on the interaction. Earlier in my use of Copilot, some of its preferred instruction-file patterns differed from the conventions used by tools such as Claude Code. Supporting more than one assistant could therefore mean maintaining overlapping instruction files or adapting a project to several competing conventions. Copilot has since become more compatible with patterns adopted across agentic development tools, but that experience showed me that some apparent model limitations were actually limitations of the surrounding product.

This distinction was also relevant during the research conversation. Copilot produced useful explanations, but a clear and confident answer should not be confused with a verified answer. Its response was most valuable when I used it interactively: asking follow-up questions, requesting comparisons, and connecting the explanation to scenarios I already understood. It was less valuable as a final authority. Claims about performance, cost, accuracy, or the suitability of one approach over another still require verification against course materials, documentation, or other reliable sources. This aligns with the course text's guidance that AI-generated output should always be reviewed for errors and inaccuracies (zyBooks, n.d., Section 1.8). The quality of the result also depended heavily on the specificity of my prompt. Broad questions produced broad explanations, while prompts that named criteria such as interpretability, data requirements, and computational cost encouraged more meaningful comparisons.

Copilot is also not necessarily the best harness for every model or every workflow. A model provider can optimize its own coding environment around the strengths of its flagship model, while Copilot must provide a common experience across models from multiple providers. Copilot's integration and flexibility are significant strengths, but there may be tradeoffs in model-specific features, usage limits, and cost. For that reason, I view Copilot as a strong and practical option rather than an all-purpose solution. Its real value depends on the model selected, the context supplied, the tools enabled, and the user's ability to question and verify its output.

## Conclusion

This activity expanded my understanding in two ways. First, it clarified that deep learning is a subset of machine learning and that different model families exist because AI problems have different data, constraints, and goals. Second, it reinforced that an AI assistant should be evaluated as a complete system rather than only by the name of its underlying model. GitHub Copilot was effective because it helped me compare ideas quickly and exposed a gap in how I had been thinking about models. Its limitations were equally instructive: the interaction depended on the harness, my prompts, and my willingness to validate the response. I left the activity with more questions than I started with, but in a productive way.
